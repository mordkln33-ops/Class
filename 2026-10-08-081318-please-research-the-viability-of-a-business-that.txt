  2. Is the market there?

  - About 17–27% of US small businesses still have no website. Clutch's 2025
    survey of owners found 17%, and other estimates run higher. Adoption is
    lowest among hair salons (63%), accountants (68%) and electricians (72%).
  - The main reason they hold out is no longer cost. Most say a website "isn't
    relevant to my industry," and only about 16% cite cost. Being cheaper won't
    win them over; you have to show them a website brings in calls.
  - The bigger market is the roughly 80% who already have a site that's slow,
    out of date, or a DIY Wix site they've given up on.
  - Prices: a competent freelancer typically charges $800–$2,500 for a small
    site, and $1,500–$8,000 is the common freelance range. Agencies charge
    $5,000–$35,000 and up. DIY builders cost $200–$600 a year, and AI builders
    start around $3–$12 a month.

  Realistic first-year math: about 12 clients at about $1,000 each comes to
  roughly $12k. That's a solid side business but not a salary. What makes a web
  business sustainable is recurring revenue, which your current "$0 monthly"
  positioning leaves out. I address that in the pricing below.

  3. Who to target

  "Small businesses" is too broad to market to. Pick one or two of these:

  1. Your own community's local businesses. The R"H Flights app suggests you're
     part of a close-knit community. Shuls, schools, simcha vendors, caterers,
     tutors and neighborhood shops all buy through referrals, which is the
     cheapest way to get clients and fits a new freelancer.
  2. Trades with low website adoption, such as electricians, cleaners,
     contractors and salons. They respond to "more calls," not "nice design."
  3. Tutors, tutoring centers and small schools. This niche is fairly open, and
     your math apps are already a portfolio for it. You could sell a website
     plus an interactive practice tool, which almost no web freelancer offers.

  6. How to get your first clients

  1. Make your portfolio honest. Harbor & Hearth, Northline Electric and Willow
     & Finch link to #. If they're concept sites, build them out and label them
     "Concept project." A prospect who clicks a dead link loses trust.
  2. Get 3 real clients quickly. Do steeply discounted or free sites for a
     nonprofit, your shul or school, and a business owned by family or friends.
     In exchange, get a testimonial, permission to list them, and a credit link
     in the footer.
  3. Find leads. Search Google Maps in your area for one niche and list
     businesses with no website, or with a slow or broken one.
  4. Use a "spec preview." For your top prospects, spend about 1–2 hours
     building a homepage mockup with their real name and services. Send it along
     with their current speed score. Cap the time per prospect, because some
     won't reply.
  5. Build referral partners. Accountants, sign and print shops, business-card
     designers and bookkeepers all meet new businesses before those businesses
     need a website. Offer them a referral fee (about 10%).
  6. Get a review after every launch. Ask for a Google review and the names of
     two businesses they know who need a site.
  7. Raise prices after 5 launches and drop the early-client prices.

  Is there a market? Yes, but only with the right framing. Selling
  general-purpose math tools to teachers would be hard, because free tools like
  GeoGebra (used by more than 100 million people), Desmos, Polypad and PhET are
  very good. The opening is in what those free tools can't offer:

  - Branding and teaching style. A tutoring center can't put its name on
    GeoGebra, and a free tool doesn't follow a particular tutor's method. Big
    tutoring brands already use free resources to attract parents; a center with
    its own tool goes a step further.
  - Interactive tools bring in more inquiries than plain pages. Most published
    numbers come from vendors and measure different things, so treat them as
    direction rather than precise figures. They consistently show quizzes,
    calculators and assessments outperforming static content.
  - This work pays more than websites. Freelance e-learning developers charge
    roughly $50–90 an hour, and a custom-coded interactive activity typically
    runs about $750–4,000. Your tools sit in that range, and few freelancers can
    build them.

  So making the tools the headline is the right call. It's a stronger
  differentiator than websites alone.
