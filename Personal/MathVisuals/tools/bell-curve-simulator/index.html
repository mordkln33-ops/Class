How it works: list every possible outcome of one try as a group (for a coin: Heads and Tails) and give each one a probability.

How it works: builds a perfect bell curve. Each dot is one member of the sample (for example, one man).

The lowest and highest numbers are 3 SDs below and above the mean — almost everyone (99.7%) is in this range. The mean is in the middle, and 1 SD = range ÷ 6.

Teacher notes

Watching a normal distribution build itself

The Bell Curve Simulator drops one dot at a time until the familiar bell shape appears, then labels the mean, median and standard deviation on the same picture. Students see where the 68-95-99.7 rule comes from instead of memorizing it.

Two ways to simulate

Press Simulate and choose a tab:

  • Single: list every outcome of one try, such as Heads 1/2 and Tails 1/2, check the outcome to count, and set the sample size and number of runs. Each dot is one run, for example “how many heads in 50 flips.” Results can be shown as a count or a percentage.
  • Bell Curve: enter what is being measured, such as Height of Adult Men, with the lowest and highest values. These are treated as three standard deviations below and above the mean, and the tool builds a perfect normal distribution with one dot per person.

Read the numbers

The panel on the left lists the mean, median, standard deviation, a 95% margin of error, the range, and the lowest and highest results. The How? buttons open a step-by-step explanation of how the mean, median and standard deviation were calculated from this data, using the actual numbers.

Find the percent in any range

In the Range Finder, type a from-and-to range or drag across the chart. The dots inside light up and the tool reports the percent and count in that range, with the z-scores of each edge. The ±1 SD, ±2 SD and ±3 SD buttons show the empirical rule right away: about 68%, 95% and 99.7%.

Teaching tip: run the same coin-flip simulation with 20 runs, then 200, then 2,000. The shape gets smoother and the margin of error shrinks. It's a memorable way to show why larger samples give more reliable results.

Frequently asked questions

How many dots can it draw?

Up to 5,000 runs in Single mode and a sample of up to 10,000 in Bell Curve mode. Use Skip to jump straight to the finished picture during the animation.

Is the simulation random?

Single mode is random: every run is a new set of simulated tries, so Run again gives a slightly different picture each time. Bell Curve mode builds an ideal normal distribution from the values you enter.

What grade levels is it for?

Middle school statistics (center and spread) through high school and AP Statistics (normal distributions, z-scores, simulation and margin of error).
