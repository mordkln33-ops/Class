How it works

  1. Type one fraction. The denominator copies to the other fraction, and the second numerator fills in as the complement — the two chances always total one whole.
  2. Name both outcomes and press Build grid. Each square is one equally likely result, painted in one of two contrasting colors.
  3. Do it again on the other side for a second experiment.
  4. Press Manipulate and pick an operation. Add answers or — either one happens. Multiply answers and — both happen together.
  5. Every result is one scenario. The two spaces are independent experiments, so pairing them gives a grid of Space A across by Space B down, and every square in it is equally likely. The chances are never just added as fractions.
  6. Every square is named with its full outcome — Heads, Red · Heads, Blue · Tails, Red · Tails, Blue. The squares that do not count toward the answer have a line struck through their name.
  7. Read the blend. Squares where both chosen outcomes happen are the two colors mixed, carrying both labels separated by a comma.

Colors are re-rolled every time you build, and are always kept pale so the dark labels stay readable on a projector.

Teacher notes

Seeing compound probability: AND, OR and the sample space

Why do we multiply for “and” but add, then subtract the overlap, for “or”? The Probability Manipulator answers that with a picture: two simple experiments become one grid of equally likely outcomes, and the answer is just the squares that count.

How to use it in class

  1. Build Space A. Enter one probability, such as 1/2, and name both outcomes, such as Heads and Tails. The second fraction fills in automatically so the two always add up to one whole. Press Build grid.
  2. Build Space B the same way, for example a spinner that lands on Red 1/4 of the time and Blue 3/4.
  3. Press Manipulate. Choose Add (or) or Multiply (and), then pick one outcome from each space. A live preview shows the equation before you commit.
  4. Show the result. A full-screen grid pairs every outcome of A with every outcome of B. Each square is labeled with its full outcome, such as “Heads, Red,” and squares that don't count are struck through.

Multiplying: P(A and B)

For independent events, the squares where both chosen outcomes happen are painted in the blended color of the two. For Heads (1/2) and Red (1/4), that's 1 square out of 8, matching P(A) × P(B) = 1/8. Students can count it.

Adding: P(A or B)

The “or” result paints every square where at least one chosen outcome happens. The legend splits that region into three pieces that don't overlap: only A, only B, and both. It shows why the addition rule subtracts P(A and B): otherwise the overlap would be counted twice.

Projector tip: the outcome colors are new every time you build a grid, but they are always pale enough for the dark labels to stay readable on a washed-out projector. If two colors look too similar, just press Build grid again.

Frequently asked questions

What denominators can I use?

Any whole number from 1 to 20. Each space has two outcomes whose probabilities share that denominator and add to 1.

Does the tool assume the events are independent?

Yes. The two spaces are treated as separate experiments, such as a coin and a spinner, so pairing them gives a joint sample space of every combination. This is the setting for the multiplication rule taught in grade 7 and high school statistics.

Is it free to use with my class?

Yes. It's free, needs no account, and runs in any modern browser on a smartboard, laptop, Chromebook or tablet.
